# Crystalize Composition

## Extraction Strength: High

Identify approximately 2–5 visual anchors. Keep only what is necessary for recognition, atmosphere, or visual storytelling:

- **Primary:** the dominant subject or silhouette.
- **Secondary:** only forms that explain or support it.
- **Atmospheric:** a light, color field, mist, or other cue that carries mood.
- **Incidental:** remove aggressively.

Do not give every detected region equal status.

## Landscape Fidelity: Medium

The photo is a reference, not a fixed grid, but retain the source's essential
spatial story. Simplify it into layered sky, land, water, architecture, or
other anchor shapes. Crop, merge, flatten, and remove detail without replacing
the landscape with an unrelated crystal form.

## Orientation Lock

Match the source orientation unless the user explicitly requests otherwise:
portrait → portrait, landscape → landscape. Do not switch to portrait merely
because “poster” is mentioned, and do not force a portrait subject into a wide
template. Preserve orientation while freely rebuilding the internal layout.

## Default Landscape Format

Create one flat, irregular-edged vignette on warm cream paper. Use roughly
55–75% of the available span and preserve generous blank margins.
Build the perimeter from interlocking facet tips, angular planes, transparent
projections, and occasional small stepped notches. Keep every edge hard,
clean, and optically readable against the paper. Do not feather, blur, fade,
smudge, airbrush, or imitate watercolor bleed. Also avoid a rectangle, hard
single-shape mask, sticker outline, floating island, crystal chunk, mineral
specimen, isometric scene, or 3D diorama.

## Authored Editorial Recomposition

Do not reuse the photo as a fixed placement map. Create a new composition from
the extracted anchors by changing at least two of: scale, spacing, crop,
overlap, shape, or negative-space pattern. Favor the concise visual rhythm of
an art magazine, travel journal, or sketchbook plate: a few memorable forms,
open edges, quiet paper, and selective asymmetry. Avoid both literal scene
translation and generic poster devices such as mastheads, halos, frames,
credits, or decorative copy.

Objects and still lifes follow the same rule. Simplify them into unequal anchor
shapes and material layers rather than tracing every photographed surface.

## Negative Space

Empty space is an active compositional element. Use it to isolate the focal subject, establish calm, and reveal the crystalline silhouette. Never feel obligated to fill the original frame.

## Selective Material Coverage

Assign treatment by hierarchy: primary anchors may be fully crystalline; secondary forms may be semi-crystalline, matte, simplified, atmospheric, or omitted. Background elements are usually quiet and minimally crystalline.

## Paper Field

Default to a matte warm ivory, cream, or pale neutral field with subtle paper
grain. Improve pale-subject legibility through restrained value separation,
denser inner facets, and soft transmitted color—not by automatically switching
to a dark background. Use a dark field only when explicitly requested or
clearly required by the source.

## Caption Zone

Reserve clean space beneath the vignette for a centered 2–4 word title or a
verified landmark/location name. Use distinctive classical handwritten script
with restrained flourishes. Keep it small—about 40–55% of vignette width and
no more than 3–4% of canvas height—typographically quiet and subordinate.

## Final Test

Does the output preserve source orientation? Can the image be recognized from
its few anchors while clearly using a newly authored layout? Does the perimeter
end in crisp crystal planes rather than watercolor haze? Does it read as a
partial editorial/journal illustration with obvious glass clarity? If it
resembles a traced photo, formal poster, flat animation, or 3D crystal object,
recompose it.
